## 2. Why Web Design?

When you think of web design, many terms come to mind: **aesthetics, interface, HTML/CSS, typography, graphics, content development, information architecture**. Web design and development matter because a website is often the primary face of an organization and the primary channel of service delivery.

## 3. Bad vs. Good Web Design

### Characteristics of bad websites

- ⚠️ Difficult to **navigate**
- ⚠️ Components on the page interfere with each other; no aesthetic sense
- ⚠️ Poor **functionality** — not easy to use from the end user's perspective
- ⚠️ Missing images, jarring/overly bright color overdoses, cluttered layouts

### Characteristics of good websites (example: Zillow.com)

- ✅ **Clean and simple** — easy to use and usable
- ✅ Simple, specific wording matched to user goals (buy / rent / sell)
- ✅ Search front and center; sign-in and key menu items clearly visible
- ✅ Works equally well on **mobile** — same functionality, adapted layout

## 4. Fundamentals of Good Web Design

### 4.1 Purpose

⭐ A website must have a **purpose** — not just be a repository for information. The purpose should be tied to the **goals of the organization** (library, archive, business, or a personal site: what do you want to achieve in the next 2–5 years?).

For a library, the primary purpose — **providing services to patrons** — must be front and center in the design.

Typical purposes: to **inform**, to **educate**, to **persuade** (e.g., buy something, join a cause).

### 4.2 Influences on design

| Influence | Examples |
|-----------|----------|
| Technology | Browsers, devices, connection speeds |
| Target audience | Who the site is for |
| Designer & content | Skills available, material to present |
| Money / budget | Resources for design and maintenance |
| Time & scope | Schedule and size of the project |
| Media types | Animation, images, text, video, mixed |

### 4.3 Usability

🔑 **Usability** is a key requirement: the website must be easy to use from the user's perspective — understandable, interesting, and usable in any form. What you see should be what you get; users should not have to puzzle over what to click.

⭐ Classic usability book: **Steve Krug, _Don't Make Me Think_** — a good website never requires the user to stop and think about how to use it.

### 4.4 "Rules" are only guidelines

⚠️ Web design "rules" change over time. Example: pages used to be kept **short** so all content fit on a laptop screen; now, with touch-based **mobile** browsing, long scrolling pages are normal and accepted.

💡 There is **no single right way** to create a website. The right way depends on:
- The **era/conventions** current at the time
- Your **end users** — their comfort with technology
- Their **devices and browsers**

### 4.5 Give users control (with guidance)

- Keep material centered on what users want to see and the information they need.
- Don't force one specific path — users should be able to arrive at content in **multiple ways**.
- Guide attention so users don't get lost, while preserving a **semblance of control**.

## 5. Generations of Web Design

| Generation | Character |
|------------|-----------|
| **1st** | Replacing paper — documents put online |
| **2nd** | Flashy elements — spinning/animated gimmicks on every page |
| **3rd** | Leading edge — heavy CSS styling, but content took a back seat |
| **4th** | ⭐ Content and technology **integrated** — content is the driving element, with deliberate use of **whitespace** in the design |

💡 Modern design should consciously avoid the problems of the first three generations.

## 6. Design Methods: Ad Hoc vs. Methodical

| Method | Description | When appropriate |
|--------|-------------|------------------|
| **Ad hoc ("seat of the pants")** | Based on intuition and experience rather than a plan; site thrown together quickly | Sites with a **short shelf life**; very small sites (a single event, a single class); genuine time emergencies |
| **Methodical, well-planned** | Includes planning and **quality assurance** — what this course teaches | Anything intended to last |

### Costs of ad hoc design

- ⚠️ Content gaps ("under construction" pages), outdated content (no time to interview stakeholders)
- ⚠️ Borrowed/broken scripts and sub-par techniques
- ⚠️ Poor usability — no time for **task design** or **usability testing**
- ⚠️ Quick fixes accumulate, making the site **difficult to update and maintain**

### Benefits of methodical design

✅ A well-thought-out design, tested before launch, saves problems in the long run: more effective, understandable, easy to navigate, and far easier to maintain.

## 7. Pre-Design Work (Requirements Gathering)

### 7.1 Steps

1. **Consider the mission** of your organization.
2. **Define the target audience.**
   - ⚠️ A common mistake: building a one-size-fits-all website for everyone — it ends up working for no one.
   - ✅ Know your target audience and map the site to that user base; if it also works for others, that's a bonus.
3. **Set goals** — both quick wins and long-term objectives.
4. **Gather content** and **chunk it into logical units.**
   - 💡 Analogy: a room full of randomly placed furniture vs. one organized into a seating area, dining area, and desk — logical grouping makes everything usable.
   - Decide: single page or multiple pages?
5. **Create an outline / plan for your content.**

### 7.2 Design on paper first

⭐ **The best design happens on paper.** Before touching software, sketch with pencil and paper:

- The **menu structure**
- What **pages** the site should contain
- How to **label** the parts of the site
- How the user's **attention should flow** from one part to another (sequencing)

Then produce the actual **sitemap** (PDF/graphic) showing the size and needs of the website and the **hierarchy of pages**, so the development team understands how users will access and how files are organized.

## 8. Influence of Technology on Design

Think through your users' technical context:

| Factor | Considerations |
|--------|----------------|
| **Browser** | Chrome, Firefox, IE/Edge — different market shares and user bases; which standards will you target? |
| **Operating system** | Windows, macOS, Linux, mobile OSes |
| **Connection speed** | Varies across US states and around the world |
| **Screen size** | 5-inch phone → laptop → 75-inch television — where is the browsing happening? |

## 9. Content Drives Design

🔑 **Content drives how the website looks.** Different audiences need different site designs:

| Audience | Design emphasis |
|----------|-----------------|
| **Prospective** clients/students | Impressive, persuasive presentation |
| **Current** clients/employees | Functional — quickly finding what they need |
| E-commerce (e.g., Amazon) | Action-oriented purchase flows |
| Government / archives / libraries | Information-rich, but can still add action items: *borrow a book, ask a librarian, listen to Internet radio* |

### Content quality practices

- ✅ Use **quality content from subject matter experts** — they know best what belongs on the site; talk to them during design.
- ✅ Have different people **periodically review** the site.
- ✅ Proofread for **grammar**; have people *unfamiliar* with the site review it to catch what you missed.

### Designer view vs. user view

⚠️ **University website problem (classic example):** what the institution puts on the homepage vs. what users actually look for:

| What the front page shows | What users actually want |
|---------------------------|--------------------------|
| Campus photos / slideshows | Faculty phone numbers and names |
| News, press releases | Campus address, application forms |
| Letter from the president | Academic calendar, maps, course listings |

⭐ **Design from the user's point of view, not the designer's** — not because you have content to showcase, but because users have tasks to accomplish.

## 10. Economic Considerations

- **Budget** for creation and for ongoing **maintenance**
- **Staff time** available (creation + upkeep)
- **In-house vs. outsourcing** the design work

All of these factor into design decisions *before* you actually begin mapping the site.

## 11. Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Usability** | How easy and pleasant a site is to use from the end user's perspective |
| **Target audience** | The specific user group a website is designed to serve |
| **Requirements gathering** | Pre-design work of identifying mission, audience, goals, and content needs |
| **Ad hoc design** | Quick, intuition-based site creation with no formal plan or QA |
| **Methodical design** | Planned design process including quality assurance and testing |
| **Sitemap** | A diagram of a site's page hierarchy and organization for the development team |
| **Content chunking** | Organizing content into logical units/groups |
| **Whitespace** | Deliberate empty space in a design (hallmark of 4th-generation design) |
| **Responsive / mobile-first** | Designing so a site works on mobile devices first, then scales up |
| **_Don't Make Me Think_** | Steve Krug's usability classic: users should never have to puzzle over an interface |
| **Subject matter expert (SME)** | The person who knows the content domain best; source of quality content |
