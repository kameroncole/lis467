## 1. The Elements of User Experience

The lecture presents a well-known chart of the **levels of web design** (the *Elements of User Experience* model). The web can be viewed two ways:

| Perspective | View of the web |
|-------------|----------------|
| **Web as software interface** | Focus on **tasks** and **functionality** |
| **Web as hypertext system** | Focus on **information** and **content** |

The planes move from **abstract → concrete** (bottom to top) and from **conception → completion** of the project:

| Plane | Software interface side | Hypertext / information side |
|-------|------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Surface** (most concrete) | **Visual design** — look and feel, treatment of graphics and interface elements | Visual design |
| **Skeleton** | **Interface design** — interface elements that enable functionality | **Navigation design** — how users move through information · **Information design** — presenting information to facilitate understanding |
| **Structure** | **Interaction design** — how users interact with parts of the site (e.g., useful micro-animations) | **Information architecture** — how the site is structured; how items are organized, named, and labeled |
| **Scope** | **Functional specifications** — detailed description of the functionality the site must include to meet user needs | **Content requirements** — what content must be on the site |
| **Strategy** (most abstract) | **Site objectives** — business, creative, or other goals · **User needs** — learned via user research, interviews, ethnography, etc. | Same |

⭐ Every website design must consider **user needs**, **site objectives**, **content**, **architecture/structure**, and **look and feel**. As you progress, you move up the planes — from design into development.

## 2. Qualities of a Good Website (UX Honeycomb)

A site you design must be:

| Quality | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| ⭐ **Useful** | Serves a real purpose for the user — even entertainment is a purpose. A beautiful site that isn't useful won't get return visits |
| **Usable** | **Easy to use**; makes **intuitive sense** |
| **Findable** | Users can **find the information** on the site easily |
| **Accessible** | Meets the needs of **people with disabilities** — more than a minimal segment of the population |
| **Desirable** | Users appreciate and are drawn to the content and design |
| **Valuable** | Content is worth something to users (e.g., the *Best Websites for Teaching & Learning* examples) |
| **Credible** | Users **believe** what is on the site |

💡 **Universal design:** when you meet the needs of people with different disabilities, you also serve **many other people**. Universally designed websites work for a lot of people.

### Web credibility

🔑 The **Stanford Web Credibility Research** project studies what causes people to believe (or not believe) a website — including design factors, assessment strategies, and what makes sites seem credible or not. A good resource if you're interested in the credibility side of design.

## 3. Personas

🔑 A **persona** is an **imaginary but representative person** who stands for a typical member of your audience.

- Rather than saying "my audience is undergrad students" or "grad students," a persona lets you go **much deeper** into what that person needs.
- Instead of "one size fits all," you tailor content to **segments of people**, each represented by a persona.

### Real-world audience segmentation examples

| Site | Segmentation |
|------|-------------|
| **Simmons University** | Top tabs for prospective undergrads, graduate students, current students, adult learners, veterans, etc. — each gets a different view |
| **New York Public Library** | *Books, Music & Movies* → new arrivals filtered for **children**, **young adults**, etc. |

User groups might include: male/female/transgender users, single parents, graduate students, PhD candidates, retirees, etc.

## 4. What Goes Into a Persona

A persona gives a **detailed description of one representative person from each segment** of site visitors.

### Demographic profile

| Element | Examples |
|---------|---------|
| **Name** & (often) **photo** | "Joe," with a picture |
| **Demographics** | Age, country, gender, marital status |
| **Occupation / job title** | Web manager, student, faculty |
| **Education & language skills** | Degree level, languages spoken |
| **Technology & experience** | Devices used; beginner / intermediate / expert web user |
| **Quote** | A line summing up what matters most to them |

### Needs and goals

- What are their **biggest problems** they're trying to solve?
- What **needs** do they have most?
- What information are they typically **searching for**?
- What **goals and tasks** do they have in relation to your site?

### Behavior-based profile

- What do they do **online**? Active on Twitter? Which **social networks** and systems do they use?
- What kinds of information do they **share** online?
- What part of the site would they spend the **most time** on?

💡 You may create **more than one persona** — one per key segment of your target audience.

---

## 5. Why Personas Help

Personas (per Usability.gov):

- ✅ Express and **focus on major needs** of the most important user groups
- ✅ Give a **clear picture of user expectations**
- ✅ Help **prioritize** features and content
- ⭐ Ensure you truly **understand your audience** by spending time building a realistic picture of them

---

## 6. Best Practices for Developing Personas

1. **Conduct user research** to answer:
   - Who are your users and **why** are they using the system?
   - What **behaviors, assumptions, and expectations** do they have?
2. **Condense** the research, look for **themes**, and refine.
3. Make personas **as realistic as possible**.

### Example persona questions

- How did they first discover the site? Why did they decide to use it?
- Age, education, profession, experience, **technical experience**
- **Motivation** and personality
- Group/role (web manager, student, faculty)
- Physical, social, and technological **environment**

### User research techniques (covered more later)

| Technique | Purpose |
|-----------|---------|
| **Focus groups** | Group discussion about needs and reactions |
| **Individual interviews** | In-depth understanding of one user |
| **Surveys** | Broad data from many users |
| **Card sorting** (information architecture) | How users group and label content |

## Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Elements of User Experience** | Five planes: strategy, scope, structure, skeleton, surface |
| **Functional specifications** | Detailed description of features a site must include |
| **Content requirements** | Description of the content a site must contain |
| **Interaction design** | Design of how users interact with the system |
| **Information architecture (IA)** | Structuring, organizing, and labeling site content |
| **Information design** | Presenting information to facilitate understanding |
| **Navigation design** | Designing how users move through the site |
| **Visual design** | The look and feel (surface) of the site |
| **Universal design** | Designing so the product works for as many people as possible |
| **Persona** | A fictional, research-based representative user |
| **Web credibility** | The degree to which users believe a site's content |
