## 1. From Information Architecture to Layout

Once your **information architecture** is in place — you know the **structure**, how content is organized into **categories, sub-headings, and sub-sub-headings** — the next step is deciding **how to lay out the elements visually**. That is where **wireframes** come in.

---

## 2. What Is a Wireframe?

🔑 A **wireframe** is a **blueprint** for a page design: a **visual representation of one or more pages** of a website that takes the IA you've developed (so far only in writing/diagrams) into a **visual form**.

- A **two-dimensional illustration** of a page or interface that focuses on **space allocation**, **prioritization of content**, **functionalities available**, and **intended behaviors** of different parts of the page.
- ⭐ **Purpose:** communicate the **layout** of the page **without getting caught up in colors, fonts, or other design details**.

### Why wireframes help

- ✅ **Time saver** — all parties can **agree on where major items go** (header, content areas, navigation menus, photos) **before** detailed design starts.
- ✅ Get the **layout nailed down** so **major layout changes don't happen** once detailed design elements are in place.
- ✅ Bridges the gap between the **site map** and the **final visual design**.

### What wireframes typically include — and exclude

| Included | Typically **not** included |
|----------|----------------------------|
| Logo | **Typography/fonts** |
| Search field | **Color** |
| **Breadcrumbs** | **Graphics / final imagery** |
| Page titles, headings, sub-headings | Detailed visual styling |
| **Global navigation** and **local navigation** | |
| Body content areas | |
| Buttons | |
| Contact information, footer | |
| Placeholders for images (e.g., a big "hero" image) | |

### Breadcrumbs

🔑 **Breadcrumbs** show a user's **location within the larger site hierarchy** — for example, *Home › Products › Phones* — and let them step back to higher levels. A **major category** is shown with its **sub-category**.

---

## 3. Reading a Wireframe

In a typical wireframe you'll see **placeholders**: a box with an X for an image, a block for a **hero image/banner**, lines for **text**, and a **footer** area at the bottom.

- You can create wireframes for different **form factors**:
  - **Desktop / laptop** (landscape, wide)
  - **Phone** (portrait — iPhone, Android)
  - **Tablet**
- A good set of wireframes shows both the **desktop view** and the **mobile view** of the site (ties back to **mobile-first** and **responsive design**).
- 💡 Searching an image site such as **Flickr** (or the web) for *"wireframe"* — and specifically *"mobile wireframe"* — shows many examples at different levels of fidelity.

---

## 4. How to Create Wireframes

| Approach | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| **Pen/pencil and paper** | No software required; sketch the boxes and label them |
| **Online wireframing tools** | Drag-and-drop elements to build a page layout quickly; log in and start immediately (the lecture demonstrates one such web tool, **GoMockingbird**) |
| **Design software** | Many drawing and prototyping tools offer wireframe kits |

⭐ You do **not** need special software — the thinking matters more than the tool.

## 5. Case Study: Stanford Center for Internet and Society (CIS) Wireframe

The IA from Part 2 (primary navigation + utility navigation) was turned into a wireframe:

- **Top of page:** logo and Stanford Law School branding for the **Center for Internet and Society**, with **two to three lines of description** (a short tagline).
- **Utility navigation** (top): blog, multimedia, contact us, get involved, press — with the **search bar** beside it.
- **Primary/major navigation** (beneath the header): focus areas, experts, events, projects, publications, about.
- **Large image placeholder** (hero area).
- Calls to action: **getting involved**, **e-mail signup**, **social media** links, **attending events**.
- **Recent events/news** modules tied to the **focus areas**, a **featured video**, and **upcoming events**.
- **Annotations/notes** on the wireframe, e.g.:
  - The featured column will have an **image with text**; users can pick **navigation options** to see other items.
  - Text at the top should **describe "what we do"** — but may need to be trimmed.
  - A **"recently" box** could change/rotate; clicking could take users to a fuller feature.

⭐ Annotations are a standard, valuable part of a wireframe: they record **behavior and decisions** that can't be drawn.

### Why it matters

- It shows the **logical progression**: **IA → wireframe → page design**.
- You can now **imagine the actual page**, even though **colors and look-and-feel** aren't decided yet.
- You're only deciding **where service elements go** on the site.

---

## 6. Key Takeaways

- 🔑 A **wireframe** is a **layout blueprint** — space, priority, functionality — **without** color, type, or graphics.
- ✅ It lets everyone **agree on layout early** and prevents costly late layout changes.
- 🔑 Common elements: **logo, search, breadcrumbs, global/local navigation, headings, body content, buttons, footer**.
- 💡 Make **both desktop and mobile** wireframes; use **paper or online tools**.
- ⭐ Follow the sequence **use cases/scenarios → IA/site map → wireframes → visual design → HTML/CSS**.
