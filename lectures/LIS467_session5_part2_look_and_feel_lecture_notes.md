## 1. What Do We Mean by "Look and Feel"?

The lecture's graphic places the **user at the center** and **separates usability from look and feel**.

💡 **Exercise:** pause and describe what *look and feel* means to you and how it differs from *usability*.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Look and feel** | The **emotional response** and **aesthetic appeal** of a website |
| **Usability** | **Ease of use** — meeting the user's needs |

Together they create an **experience** that is **useful**, **easy to use**, **attractive**, and gives a **good emotional response**.

**Basic definition:** the look and feel of a website is **how the site looks to the user** and **how it feels when they interact with it**.

---

## 2. The "Look"

The **look** is defined by:

- **Color palette** — the colors used, how they combine, and the **contrast**. Colors that don't belong together can make a site unattractive.
- **Images**
- **Layout**
- **Font (typography) choices**
- **Overall styling**

---

## 3. The "Feel"

The **feel** is determined by:

- **Movement and response of dynamic components** — **drop-down menus, buttons, forms, galleries**. These interactions provoke a **reaction** in the user.
- **Sound effects**
- **Page-load speed** — how quickly pages load

⭐ Each of these arouses an **emotional response**. Example: an **annoying pop-up ad** appearing while you're trying to read something — the annoyance is the "feel."

## 4. Case Study: State of Michigan Web Standards

The **State of Michigan** publishes standards that guide **state agencies** in determining the look and feel of websites.

- **Purpose:** establish **design and development standards** for websites and applications so **all state applications and agency sites provide a similar experience**.
- **Scope:** applies to all applications/sites hosted within the **State of Michigan standard web content management system**.
- Agencies **must follow** a defined set of guidelines, including **accessibility** and other resource/flexibility guidelines, and the standards exist as a **formal document**.

### What the standards specify

| Area | Details from the lecture |
|---|---|
| **Header** | Must contain the **official State of Michigan brand logo, title, and tag line** |
| **Footer** | What goes in and what doesn't; technical and accessibility requirements |
| **Colors** | **Primary/accent colors** for links, backgrounds, and buttons; must meet **accessibility compliance** — e.g., for users who are **color-blind** or have visual impairments |
| **Icons** | Which icons to use on the site |
| **Typography** | Font standards |
| **Badges** | Standard badge components |
| **Breadcrumbs** | Show **which part of the site you're in**; standards say how to insert them |
| **Code samples** | Reusable HTML/CSS/JS snippets users can **copy and paste** |

- The additional guidelines live at **Michigan.gov**, with a **UI components library** (colors, icons, typography, badges, breadcrumbs).
- They embed **CodePen**-style examples (the captions say "codependent page"), an online tool where you **enter HTML/CSS/JavaScript and see the rendered result** — handy for trying out snippets.
- Standards are **updated over time** (the lecture also showed an **older 2016 version**).

### Takeaway

Although these apply specifically to Michigan, they are **good examples of standards to follow in general**. You may use your own color branding, but many **best practices** still apply.

## 5. Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Look** | Visual attributes: colors, contrast, images, layout, fonts, styling |
| **Feel** | Interaction and response: dynamic components, sound, speed |
| **Aesthetic appeal** | Visual attractiveness and emotional response |
| **Design standards** | Documented rules ensuring consistent experience across sites |
| **Breadcrumbs** | Navigation aid showing the user's location in the site |
| **Accessibility (color compliance)** | Choosing colors usable by people with visual impairments |
