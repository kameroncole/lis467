## 2. Defining Your Audience

🔑 The first question to ask about any website: **"Who is this website for?"**

In a library context, the audience might be:

- Library **staff**
- Library **patrons**
- The broader **community**

⭐ **The audience is the foundation element for everything you decide about your website.**

### Possible audience segments

| Segment | Example |
|---------|---------|
| Language | English speakers; people with English as a second language; speakers of other native languages |
| Age | Children, teens, adults |
| Role | Prospective students, current students, faculty, job seekers |

💡 **You cannot serve everyone** — but if you build a site well for a specific audience, chances are that **other people will also be served well**.

## 3. What Users Need vs. What Sites Show

The lecture shows a well-known cartoon comparing a **university website's front page** with what users actually look for:

| What's on the front page | What users actually want |
|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Marketing material, promotional photos, messages from leadership | Campus address, how to contact a faculty member, job application forms, course info, etc. |

- Marketing content may suit **prospective students**, but **current students** have very different needs.
- ✅ Solution: provide **audience-based tabs or paths** at the top (e.g., *Prospective Students*, *Current Students*, *Visitors*). Selecting one leads to a page tailored to that audience.

## 4. The Web Coordinator Role in Libraries

A quoted source on **library web management** describes the division of responsibility:

- The **web librarian / assistant** may be in charge of *maintaining* the website — updating pages and adhering to standards — but **not the content**.
- **Content** is often managed by individual **library departments**, frequently without coordinated direction.
- ⭐ Therefore a **web coordinator** is needed who can work with stakeholders across the library to create a **cohesive site structure and content**.

🔑 Deciding *what* content belongs on the site depends on **understanding who the audience is**.

## 5. Design Considerations: Good vs. Bad Library Websites

### ⚠️ Examples of poor design

| Site | Problem |
|------|---------|
| A memorial library website | **Too busy** — too many items competing for attention at once |
| A public library website | **Looks very dated** — "like a website from the nineties" |

### ✅ Examples of good design

| Site | What it does well |
|------|------------------|
| **Marin County Free Library** | Clean and neat; **audience tabs** (Kids, Teens, etc.) leading to age-specific pages with story times and events |
| **Boston Public Library** | Find books by **format**; **Services for…** menu by audience — English learners, job seekers, seniors, **users with disabilities** (accessible resources and services) |
| **Phoenix Public Library** | Pages for **Kids** and **Teens**; services overview and event boxes linking to more info. The top area is a little busy, but the rest is fine |

💡 Good library sites **segment by audience** so each group quickly finds what is relevant to them.

## 6. Evaluating Websites: The CRAAP Test

🔑 The **CRAAP test** is a set of criteria for evaluating whether a website is good, bad, or credible:

| Letter | Criterion | Questions to ask |
|--------|-----------|------------------|
| **C** | **Currency** | When was it created? When was it last updated? Is that date shown? |
| **R** | **Relevance** | Who is the intended audience? Is the content relevant to your need? |
| **A** | **Authority** | Who is the author? Are they associated with a credible organization? Is author/contact information available? |
| **A** | **Accuracy** | Is the information supported by sources? Do other reliable sites confirm it? |
| **P** | **Purpose** | Why does the site exist — to inform, sell, persuade, entertain, or deceive? |

### Worked example (hoax-style site)

The lecture walks through an evaluation of a sample site:

- **Currency:** created 1998, updated in later years — dates are displayed ✅
- **Relevance:** audience appears to be people interested in preserving endangered species; but the site also runs an online store selling merchandise ⚠️
- **Authority:** the author's name links to a personal website; the site is **not associated** with a school, educational, or recognized organization ⚠️
- **Accuracy:** most sources on the subject point back to the author's own blog or similar sites; independent sources say the opposite ⚠️

⚠️ Another example site uses **Flash** — not a good idea, as many browsers discourage or no longer support Flash.

💡 Apply the same CRAAP criteria to any website you evaluate.

## 8. Usable Library Websites

🔑 **Usability** = the site is **easy to use** and meets users' needs in various ways (covered in more depth in later lectures).

| Example | Why it's usable |
|---------|----------------|
| A **college library** site (instructor's favorite) | Very **clean**; a menu icon reveals more detail; visit/plan options; **feels like the physical library** — "an extension of the physical space"; hours and contact info visible; **not too much text at once** |
| **Garfield County Libraries** | Clear, sensible structure |
| **Montana State University Library** | More information, but still a prominent **search** bar and clear steps/services for users |
| **Queens Public Library** | Programs and services organized for different audiences |

⭐ Common pattern: **clean layout, prominent search, audience-specific paths, limited text per screen.**

## Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Primary audience** | The main group a website is designed to serve |
| **Audience segmentation** | Dividing users into groups (age, role, language) with distinct needs |
| **Web coordinator** | Person who coordinates site structure and content across departments |
| **CRAAP test** | Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose — website evaluation criteria |
| **Usability** | How easy a site is to use and how well it meets users' needs |
| **Flash** | Obsolete browser plug-in technology; discouraged on modern sites |
